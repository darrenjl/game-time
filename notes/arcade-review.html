01The verdict

The craft is consistently good. Every game runs clean — no console errors anywhere, no broken layouts at either iPad orientation, a shared top bar, and a difficulty picker that behaves the same way in ten different games. That consistency is real work and it shows.

The problem isn't quality, it's variety and ceiling. Twenty-three games use four verbs between them: tap a target, tap to jump, swipe a direction, drag a thing. Six games are three games wearing different hats. And apart from Block Craft, nothing in the library has a goal you can finish, a reason to come back tomorrow, or anything a six-year-old can get better at in a way the game acknowledges.

Finley is well served — most of these are ideal for a three-year-old. Oisín is the one who has outgrown the shelf.

The clearest case

Peekaboo Meadow and High-Five Foxes are byte-identical in their difficulty tables — 2.20 / 1.90, 1.15 / 0.95, 0.85 / 0.72, 0.60 / 0.52. Side by side on screen, only the animal in the hole differs.

Why it matters less than it sounds

Two skins of a game a toddler loves is not a crime — Finley will happily play both. The cost is shelf space: they take two of twenty-three tiles, and neither gives Oisín anything the other didn't.

03Things that are actually broken

Two real defects, found by reading the code rather than playing.

Copy Cat and Soccer Stars fight over the same settingBug

Both games store their difficulty in localStorage under the key ss_diff. Every game is served from the same origin, so they share one storage area — the key is not scoped per game.

Set Copy Cat to ⚡Zoom (index 3) and Soccer Stars silently falls back to Easy, because it only has three options. Set Soccer Stars to Hard (index 2) and Copy Cat becomes Fast. Neither crashes — both guard the lookup — so it presents as "the difficulty won't stay where I put it". Shape Sort already uses ss2_*, which suggests this collision was hit once before and half-fixed.

Diamond & Crystal Hunt punishes the way children play mazesDesign

Five bumps into a wall ends the run — and "Try Again" generates a brand new maze rather than replaying the one they were learning. Bumping into walls is how a small child reads a maze; the game treats exploration as failure, then deletes the map they'd built in their head.

In testing, eight undirected taps were enough to trigger it.

04Usability, across the whole library

Thirteen games make no sound whatsoeverBiggest cheap win

Reef Rescue, Build-a-Buddy, Fruit Rain, Caterpillar Hop, Doodle, Chomp!, High-Five Foxes, Jumping Adventure, Lane Runner, Ocean Match, Octopus Treasure Hunt, Peekaboo Meadow and Shape Sort never create an AudioContext. A whack-a-mole with no boing, a fruit-slicer with no slice, a memory game with no chime.

For this age group sound isn't decoration — it's the feedback that tells a child the tap worked. The tone() helper in Block Craft and Copy Cat is about fifteen lines and drops straight in.

Only two games can be mutedParent-facing

Bob the Destructor and Potty Crew have a 🔊 toggle. The other eight noisy games have no way to quieten them — no setting, and nothing that survives to the next game. On a shared sofa that is the difference between a game being allowed and not.

Every game opens with a paragraph neither boy can readSystemic

Between 29 and 48 words of English prose on each start screen. "Ozzie the mole, Finn the frog and friends peek out of the burrows! Tap a friend to give them a high-five. Don't tap the grumpy bee!" Finley can't read any of it; Oisín can decode it slowly, which is not the same as reading it.

The instructions are there for you, not for them — which is fine, but it means the games are currently taught by a parent rather than by the game. A three-second animated demo loop on the start screen (a finger tapping a mole, a bee shaking its head) would teach both boys without a word.

Two games still use a D-padConsistency

The two maze games are the oldest in the library and steer with four on-screen arrows. Everything built since is swipe-or-tap. They're also the only two games with no pause and no difficulty setting.

05Fun and depth — the real gap

This is where the six-year-old is underserved, and it's structural rather than a matter of polish.

Nothing to finish

Four games have a win state: the two mazes, Ocean Match, and Block Craft's quest chain. Everything else is an endless loop that stops when you lose. There is no "I completed it", no last level, no ending to reach and tell someone about.

Nothing of theirs survives being closed

Correction: this section first claimed Build-a-Buddy forgets the buddy. It doesn't — it stores bb_body, bb_color, bb_hat and bb_treat through a save() wrapper my first pass didn't match. Doodle was the only game that genuinely lost a child's work, and it now keeps the drawing.

Nineteen of the twenty-two games keep nothing but a best score. Block Craft's world, the Potty Crew's decorated potty, Build-a-Buddy's buddy and now Doodle's drawing are the places where a child's own work is still there the next day.

This is not an argument for rewards, badges or collectables — those are retention mechanics, and they belong in the apps you're building an alternative to. It's simpler than that: a six-year-old who spends ten minutes making something reasonably expects it to still exist, and losing it teaches him the opposite. Block Craft holds attention not because it pays him to return but because his house is where he left it.

Nothing to plan

Almost every game is reactive: something appears, you touch it. Nothing asks Oisín to make a choice whose consequence arrives later — no resource to spend, no route to pick, no order to do things in, no risk to weigh. That's precisely the kind of thinking that comes online around six, and the library never exercises it.

Nothing that fails softly

Losing generally means starting from zero. Nothing carries progress forward between attempts, so a hard game gets abandoned rather than practised.

The four verbs

Tap a target · tap to jump · swipe a direction · drag an object. Absent: aiming with force and angle, drawing a path, sequencing moves before they run, rhythm, turn-taking, anything two-handed.

What Block Craft proves

It's the one game with depth, choice and persistence — and it's also the biggest by a factor of two. Depth costs code. It doesn't have to cost this much code, but it isn't free.

08What I'd do, in order

Cheapest and highest-value first. The first three are an evening each.

  1. Rename Soccer Stars' storage key.Done — sst_diff. Every other key in the library was checked for the same trap; none collide.
  2. Give the silent games a voice.Done — twelve games gained sound (the thirteenth, High-Five Foxes, was merged away). Verified by counting oscillators in a real browser, game by game.
  3. Put a mute in the shared top bar.Done — one 🔊 button in all 22 games on a shared gt_mute key, so muting anywhere mutes everywhere.
  4. Soften Diamond Hunt.Done — bumping a wall is now just a bonk. The run never ends, so the maze they were learning is never taken away.
  5. Teach the game without words.Done both ways — every game has a 🗣️ button that reads its own instructions aloud via speechSynthesis (emoji stripped, respects mute), and the four games with a non-obvious swipe or drag loop a silent ghost-hand demo behind the start screen.
  6. Merge one of the duplicate pairs.Done — High-Five Foxes is now the Den cast inside Peekaboo Meadow, with its own dusk palette, foxes and rats. The grid is 22 tiles.
  7. Let their work survive being closed.Doodle should keep the drawing. Build-a-Buddy should keep the buddy. Not a reward for coming back — just their own thing still being there, the way Block Craft's world is. Three or four localStorage writes in total.
  8. Split Block Craft in two.Done — 🧱 Building Blocks is the simple build-and-smash toy for Finley; Block Craft keeps the world and gained a Build/Fight choice, an ℹ️ panel in the pause menu, a hero preview that shows the weapon you picked, and an escape from any hole you dig.
  9. Then build upward for Oisín.The gap is planning, sequencing and consequence — none of which the current four verbs touch. That's a new game, not a patch.
